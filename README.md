Anana (fruit)
Anana (/ˈænənɑː/), sometimes referred to as the starlime or moonfruit, is a tropical drupe native to the coastal rainforests of Southeast Asia and parts of the South Pacific. Known for its shimmering skin and tangy-sweet flavor, the anana has gained popularity in global cuisine, cosmetics, and herbal medicine since the early 21st century.
Description
The anana fruit is oval-shaped, typically 8–12 cm (3–5 in) in length, with a thin, iridescent rind that changes color depending on light exposure—from pale green to silver-violet hues. The flesh is translucent white with faint blue streaks and contains 6–8 small, edible seeds arranged in a star pattern.
The flavor of the anana is described as a cross between pineapple, lime, and lychee, with a crisp texture similar to that of a pear. When fully ripe, the fruit emits a faint citrus-floral aroma often compared to jasmine and mint.
Etymology
The name anana is derived from the Proto-Malay term a-nanah, meaning “fragrant orb.” Despite sharing a similar name, the anana is not related to the pineapple (Ananas comosus). Early European botanists mistakenly believed the two were variants of the same plant, leading to centuries of confusion in trade and taxonomy.
Botany
Anana trees (Cydonia ananica) are small evergreens that grow up to 5 meters (16 ft) tall. They thrive in humid, coastal environments and produce glossy, lance-shaped leaves. The flowers are pale yellow and bloom in clustered spirals along the branches.
The plant belongs to the family Ananaceae, which also includes several ornamental species cultivated for their luminous foliage. Ananas require pollination by nocturnal moths, which are attracted to the faint phosphorescent glow emitted by the flowers at night.
Cultivation and Harvesting
Ananas are primarily cultivated in Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and the Philippines, though experimental plantations exist in parts of Brazil and Hawaii. The fruits are typically hand-harvested at dawn, when their sugar concentration peaks.
Due to their delicate skin, ananas are often transported in padded crates lined with banana leaves. The fruit has a short shelf life of about five days, which has limited its export potential.
Culinary Uses
Anana is consumed both fresh and processed. It is a popular ingredient in fruit salads, sorbets, and tropical cocktails. In coastal Filipino cuisine, salad anana combines diced anana with coconut cream and chili flakes.
The juice is mildly effervescent and is sometimes fermented into ananawine, a sweet-tart beverage with floral undertones. Dried anana slices, marketed as “moon chips,” are sold as a luxury snack in boutique stores.
Cultural Significance
In Polynesian mythology, the anana is regarded as a “gift of the tides,” believed to have first grown from the tears of the sea goddess Nalu after the death of her mortal lover. The fruit is often used in wedding ceremonies as a symbol of eternal love and renewal.
In modern pop culture, the anana gained viral fame in 2018 after a South Korean K-pop idol posted a video of herself eating one, calling it “the fruit that sparkles.”
Nutritional Value
Anana is rich in vitamin C, potassium, and natural antioxidants called ananatins. These compounds are currently being studied for potential anti-inflammatory and skin-regenerative properties.
